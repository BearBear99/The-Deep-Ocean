What is the deep sea?

The deep sea begins below 200 meters, where sunlight vanishes fast, pressure rises to extreme levels, and temperatures drop near freezing—typically between 0–4 °C. Despite these harsh conditions, life thrives, with around 27,000 species identified, though thousands or millions more likely remain undiscovered. Creatures here use adaptations like bioluminescence and slow metabolism to survive in diverse zones, from the mesopelagic to the hadalpelagic.

Mesopelagic — “Twilight Zone” (200–1,000 m)

Dimly lit and rapidly cooling, this zone is home to countless creatures adapted to near darkness. Many have bioluminescent organs used for camouflage or communication. Every night, billions of fish migrate upward to feed and return by day—forming the largest daily movement of life on Earth.

Pressure: like 20–100 cars stacked.
History: 1934 Bathysphere reached ~923 m.

Bathypelagic — “Midnight Zone” (1,000–4,000 m)

Sunlight never reaches this part of the ocean. The water stays just above freezing, around 2 to 4°C, and the pressure is immense — hundreds of times greater than at the surface. Life here moves slowly to save energy, depending on “marine snow,” the tiny bits of dead plankton and debris drifting down from above.

Pressure: like a skyscraper on a person.
History: 1953 Bathyscaphe Trieste reached ~3,150 m.

Species in this Zone

Abyssopelagic — “Abyss” (4,000–6,000 m)

The abyss is a world of total darkness and silence. The temperature stays just above freezing, around 2°C, and the pressure is enormous. There are no plants here, only slow-moving creatures that survive on whatever food drifts down from the upper ocean. Some deep-sea species grow unusually large — a trait known as deep-sea gigantism — to store energy and endure the cold.

Pressure: As heavy as several hundred cars pressing all at once.
History: DSV Alvin routinely explores ~4,500 m (since the 1960s).

Hadalpelagic — “Trenches” (6,000–11,000 m)

The deepest part of the ocean lies in narrow trenches that can stretch for thousands of kilometers. The temperature stays near 1°C, and the pressure is more than a thousand times higher than at sea level. Life here has evolved in isolation, with fragile, slow-growing species adapted to the silence and crushing weight.

Pressure: So powerful it could crush a car as if it were made of tin.
History: 1960 Trieste reached ~10,911 m; later 2012 and 2019 hadal dives.

Deep Sea Pollution

Studies show deep-sea litter is rapidly increasing. According to Science (2022), marine debris in the deep ocean has risen by over 60% in the past 30 years. The most common items are plastic bags, straws, and fishing nets—even plastic bags were found in the 10,000m-deep Mariana Trench. These materials take hundreds to thousands of years to decompose: a plastic bottle about 450 years, fishing nets over 600 years. The impact is severe: whales, turtles, and fish die from entanglement or ingestion, while microplastics enter the food chain, eventually returning to humans.
